    HASHING · MODULE 14

    SHA-256

    Intermediate

    In this lesson you will learn:

    • How SHA-256 scales up SHA-1's idea: more state, more rounds, bigger security margin
    • Why it remains trusted today with no known practical collision
    • The most important lesson in the whole hashing category: 'secure hash' ≠ 'safe for passwords'
    • Why a stronger algorithm alone never fixes a weak, guessable password

    Introduction

    SHA-256 is where the "one-way digest" story currently lands for general-purpose hashing: no known practical collisions, a wide 256-bit output, and it's everywhere — TLS certificates, Git's newer hash option, Bitcoin's proof-of-work, and software/firmware signing. If MD5 and SHA-1 taught you what breaks, SHA-256 is what to actually reach for.

    History

    SHA-256 is part of the SHA-2 family, designed by the NSA and published by NIST in 2001 as the intended replacement once SHA-1's long-term security was already being questioned (well before SHA-1's practical break actually arrived in 2017). Unlike the awkward transition from MD5 to SHA-1 (both eventually broken), SHA-256 was specifically engineered with a larger internal state and more mixing to resist the same style of attack. It remains the current standard today, over two decades on, with no practical collision or preimage attack found.

    Brief definition

    DEFINITION

    SHA-256 is a hash function producing a 256-bit (32-byte, 64 hex character) digest — currently considered cryptographically secure, with no known practical way to find a collision or reverse it.

    Like MD5 and SHA-1, it's still deterministic, one-way, and keyless — the same three hashing properties carry through the whole family. What's different is scale and internal design: a bigger output, a bigger internal state, and more mixing steps, all making the brute-force and collision math dramatically harder.

    How it works

    Message→Pad to 512-bit multiple→8 working buffers (a-h)→64 rounds of mixing per 512-bit block→256-bit digest

    SHA-256 keeps eight 32-bit working variables (compare: MD5 has 4, SHA-1 has 5), and processes each 512-bit block through 64 rounds, each mixing in a message-derived word and a round constant (the first 32 bits of the fractional parts of the cube roots of the first 64 prime numbers — deliberately chosen to be "nothing up my sleeve" numbers, not secretly weakened). The larger state and more rounds are exactly what gives SHA-256 its much larger security margin over MD5/SHA-1.

    Practical tasks

    TASK 03multiple choice

    Is SHA-256 a safe choice for hashing passwords directly (no salt, no cost factor)?

    Yes — it's currently considered cryptographically secure
    No — "cryptographically secure" and "safe for passwords" are different properties; SHA-256 is still fast and unsalted by default, same problem as MD5/SHA-1

    Strengths

    • No known practical collision or preimage attack after over two decades of public cryptanalysis — currently trusted for integrity, signatures, and certificates.
    • Large security margin — 256-bit output means brute-forcing a collision by chance (a "birthday attack") would need on the order of 2^128 attempts, astronomically infeasible.
    • Fast and hardware-accelerated on modern CPUs, making it practical for high-throughput integrity checks (TLS, blockchain, file verification).

    Weaknesses

    There's no known algorithm weakness to report for SHA-256 today — that's precisely why it's still the standard.

    Implementation weakness — using it for passwords anyway: exactly like MD5 and SHA-1, SHA-256's speed (a strength for integrity-checking) becomes a liability the moment it's used to store passwords without a purpose-built, deliberately slow wrapper. Plain sha256(password), even salted, is still far faster to brute-force per-guess than bcrypt — this is a misuse of the tool, not a flaw in SHA-256 itself.

    Cybersecurity & CTF perspective

    SHA-256 underlies the integrity model of HTTPS certificates, code signing, and blockchain proof-of-work — understanding it is foundational for web security, forensics (verifying file/evidence integrity), and blockchain-adjacent security work alike. In CTFs, "crack this SHA-256 hash" challenges still work exactly like the MD5/SHA-1 wordlist attacks (the algorithm being secure doesn't stop a weak, guessable password from being found quickly) — the lesson repeats because it's the single most common real-world mistake: a strong hash function does not make a weak password strong.

    THE REAL LESSON

    Switching MD5 → SHA-256 changed nothing about brute-force cost here, because neither is designed to be slow. Only bcrypt/Argon2/scrypt (deliberately expensive, salted) actually fix this.

    Summary

    • SHA-256 produces a 256-bit digest through 64 rounds across 8 working buffers.
    • Currently has no known practical collision or preimage attack — the trusted standard today.
    • Still the wrong tool for passwords — speed makes it unsuitable regardless of its algorithmic strength.
    • This closes general-purpose hashing; bcrypt (next) is purpose-built for the one case SHA-256 isn't.
