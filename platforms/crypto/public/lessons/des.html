    DES

    Intermediate

    In this lesson you will learn:

    • What a block cipher and a Feistel network are

    Introduction

    DES is your first true block cipher — the family AES belongs to. Where XOR and RC4 worked byte-by-byte, DES processes data in fixed 64-bit chunks through a structured, repeated scrambling process called a Feistel network. Understanding DES's rounds makes AES's rounds (coming up next) far easier to follow, since the overall idea — repeat a round function many times, mixing in a different subkey each time — is shared by both.

    History

    DES ("Data Encryption Standard") was developed by IBM in the early 1970s (building on an internal cipher called Lucifer) and standardized by the US government (NBS, now NIST) in 1977. It became the first publicly available, government-endorsed encryption standard, and dominated commercial and government cryptography for two decades. Its downfall was its key: only 56 bits. As computing power grew, 56-bit keys became brute-forceable — a dedicated machine called "Deep Crack" broke a DES key in under a day in 1998. NIST formally deprecated DES and ran the open competition that produced its replacement: AES.

    Status today: Educational / Legacy / Insecure (56-bit key is brute-forceable)

    Brief definition

    DEFINITION

    DES is a symmetric block cipher: it encrypts 64-bit (8-byte) blocks using a 56-bit key (stored as a 64-bit value with 8 unused parity bits), through 16 structured rounds.

    FEISTEL NETWORK

    A cipher structure that splits each block into two halves, and in every round runs one half through a scrambling function mixed with a subkey, XORs the result into the other half, then swaps the halves. Its key property: the same structure decrypts correctly just by running the subkeys in reverse order — no separate "inverse" function needed for the round itself.

    How it works

    64-bit block→Initial Permutation (IP)→16 Feistel rounds→Swap→Final Permutation (IP⁻¹)→64-bit ciphertext

    Key schedule

    The 64-bit key first passes through PC-1 (Permuted Choice 1), which drops the 8 parity bits and permutes the remaining 56. That 56-bit value is split into two 28-bit halves, which are rotated left by 1 or 2 bits every round, then combined and compressed by PC-2 into a 48-bit subkey for that round — 16 rounds, 16 different subkeys.

    One Feistel round

    1. Split the 64-bit block into left (L) and right (R) 32-bit halves.
    2. Expansion (E) — expand R from 32 to 48 bits (duplicating some bits) so it can be XORed with the 48-bit subkey.
    3. XOR the expanded R with that round's subkey.
    4. S-boxes — 8 fixed substitution tables compress the 48 bits back down to 32, non-linearly (DES's confusion step, playing the same role AES's SubBytes does).
    5. Permutation (P) — shuffle those 32 bits.
    6. XOR the result into L, then swap L and R for the next round.

    expanded = _permute(R, E)
    mixed = expanded ^ subkey
    sboxed = _apply_sboxes(mixed)
    permuted = _permute(sboxed, P)
    return R, L ^ permuted   # new (L, R), with the swap built in

    Practical tasks

    TASK 03identify the weakness

    In one sentence: what's the single biggest practical reason DES is retired?

    Strengths

    • Historically foundational — the Feistel structure, S-boxes, and key-schedule ideas DES pioneered directly shaped how later ciphers (and cryptanalysis techniques) were designed.
    • Well-studied — decades of public cryptanalysis found no practical shortcut attack better than brute force on the key itself, which at the time (1970s hardware) was considered secure.

    Weaknesses

    Algorithm weakness — key size: 56 bits means 2^56 (about 72 quadrillion) possible keys. That sounds large, but modern hardware (and especially GPUs/FPGAs/cloud compute) can exhaust that space in hours to days. DES is obsolete for this reason alone, even though no deeper structural flaw in its rounds was ever found.

    Also algorithm-level — small block size: 64-bit blocks make DES vulnerable to "birthday bound" collision attacks (like Sweet32) once enough data is encrypted under one key — another reason block size matters, which is part of why AES uses 128-bit blocks.

    Cybersecurity & CTF perspective

    DES rarely appears in modern production systems, but it appears constantly in legacy-system audits, old hardware (ATMs, old payment terminals, some embedded devices), and CTF/educational contexts specifically because its key is small enough to brute-force within a challenge's time budget — making "just brute force the DES key" a legitimate, intended solution path rather than a shortcut. Recognizing an 8-byte block cipher with a short key as "probably DES" is a useful forensic instinct.

    REASONING

    The real key space here isn't DES's theoretical 2^56 — it's however many values the key-derivation process can actually produce. A 4-digit PIN has only 10,000 possibilities (0000–9999), so the effective keyspace is 10,000 keys, trivially brute-forced in milliseconds. The lesson: a cipher's security is capped by its weakest link, and a poorly chosen key defeats even a sound algorithm.

    Summary

    • DES encrypts 64-bit blocks through 16 Feistel rounds (expand, XOR, S-box, permute).
    • Its 56-bit key is the fatal weakness: brute-forceable on modern hardware, not a deeper structural flaw.
    • The Feistel structure's elegant property — same steps decrypt by reversing subkey order — reappears conceptually in many later ciphers.
    • Up next: Triple DES, a stopgap that reuses DES three times to patch the key-size problem.
